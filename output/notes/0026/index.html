Definition. Well-founded induction [0026]

  • November 25, 2025

Well founded induction describes the generalized induction principle to any well-founded relation \(\prec \). Given a set \(S\) with a well-founded relation \(\prec \), to prove that a property \(P(x)\) holds for all elements \(x \in S\), it suffices to show that for every element \(x \in S\), if \(P(y)\) holds for all elements \(y \in S\) such that \(y \prec x\), then \(P(x)\) also holds. Formally, this can be expressed as:

\[ (\forall x \in S.\ (\forall y \in S.\ y \prec x \to P(y)) \to P(x)) \to (\forall x \in S.\ P(x)) \]

    Definition. Structural induction [0025]

    • November 25, 2025

    Structural induction represents well-founded induction applied to the strict subformula relation. To prove that a property \(P(\phi )\) holds for all formulas \(\phi \), it suffices to show that for every formula \(\phi \), if \(P(\psi )\) holds for all strict subformulas \(\psi \) of \(\phi \), then \(P(\phi )\) also holds. Formally, this can be expressed as (1):

    \[ (\forall \phi .\ (\forall \psi .\ \psi \prec _{\text {sf}} \phi \to P(\psi )) \to P(\phi )) \to (\forall \phi .\ P(\phi )) \]

    Often times the discussion of structural induction specifically refers to (abstract) syntax where we define a finite tree with ordered branches as the structure of expressions. In practical terms this means we define an inductive type for the syntax of expressions, and then we can perform structural induction over this inductive type. When discussing SI through the lens of constructors we can rephrase the above principle as follows.

    For any property \(P(e)\) of some expression \(e\), to prove that:

    \[ \forall e \in \texttt {E}.\ P(e) \]

    it's sufficient to prove that for each tree constructor \(c\) if some arity \(k \geq 0\) (takes 0 or more args) that if \(P\) holds for all subtrees \(e_1, e_2, \ldots , e_k\) then it also holds for the tree constructed by applying \(c\) to these subtrees (2):

    \[ \forall c.\ \forall e_1,\ldots ,e_k.\ (P(e_1) \land P(e_2) \land \ldots \land P(e_k)) \to P(c(e_1, e_2, \ldots , e_k)) \to (\forall e \in \texttt {E}.\ P(e)) \]

      Definition. Well-founded Relation [0022]

      • November 25, 2025

      We say that a binary relation or predicate \(\prec \) over a set \(S\) is a well-founded relation if there does not exist an infinite sequence of elements such that each successive element is less than it's predecessor according to the relation \(\prec \). Formally this can be expressed as follows 1:

      \[ \nexists x_0, x_1, x_2, \ldots \in S.\ x_1 \prec x_0 \land x_2 \prec x_1 \land x_3 \prec x_2 \land \ldots \]

      In other words each sequence of elements of S that decreases according to the relation \(\prec \) must eventually terminate and cannot continue indefinitely. Another way to express this is that every non-empty subset \(T \subseteq S\) must have a minimal element \(m \in T\) such that there is no other element \(t \in T\) with \(t \prec m\). Here we can express this as:

      \[ (\forall T \subseteq S.\ T\ \mathrlap {\,/}{=}\ \emptyset \implies \exists m \in T.\ \forall t \in T.\ \lnot (t \prec m)) \]

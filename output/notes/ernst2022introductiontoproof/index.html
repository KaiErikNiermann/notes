    Definition. Complete (Strong) Induction [0021]

    • November 24, 2025

    If we let \(P(0), P(1), \ldots \) be a sequence of propositions indexed by the natural numbers, then the principle of complete induction (also known as strong induction) states that to prove that all of these propositions are true, it suffices to show that for every natural number \(m\), if all of the propositions \(P(0), P(2), \ldots , P(m)\) are true, then \(P(n)\) is also true where \(m < n\). In other words, we assume the truth of all previous cases up to \(m\) in order to prove the case for \(n\). The schema for the principle of complete induction is as follows (1):

    \[ \forall n.\ (\forall m.\ m < n \to P(m)) \to P(n) \]

    In this formulation the base case is implicitly included since when \(n = 0\), the inner universal quantification \(\forall m.\ m < 0 \to P(m)\) is vacuously true hence we prove \(P(0)\) directly. We can also state the more classic form of complete induction which explicitly includes the base case as follows:

    \[ \forall n.\ (\forall m.\ (m \leq n) \to P(m)) \to P(n + 1) \]

    Here the base case ends up being:

    \[ ((1 \geq m \leq 1) \to P(1)) \to P(2) \equiv P(1) \to P(2) \]

    Here our base case similarly is proven directly just by virtue of the rhs implication reducing to \(P(1)\)2. A note to make here is that conceptually there is no difference between how we proove the base case in complete induction versus ordinary induction.
